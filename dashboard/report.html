How this actually works

A card, a door, and a spreadsheet that grades itself. No statistics background assumed — every claim below is something you can watch happen on the bench.

02Attendance is a fact per day, not a counter

The first accepted tap of the day makes you present. Tap again twenty minutes later and the door still opens — you are simply walking in — but the record says ALREADY_MARKED and the attendance number does not move.

So the percentage is not “taps” and not “minutes”. It is:

attendance %  =  days you were present  ÷  days the system recorded
Aisha         =  6 / 8                                     =  75%

75% is the usual eligibility line, which is why anything below it is marked in red in the table. Nothing is punished automatically — the percentage is just arithmetic.

03What a real queue looks like, and what a stack looks like

This is the one honest weakness of any card system: a UID cannot prove who is holding the card. So instead of pretending, the system measures the rhythm of taps.

  • A real queue — one person walks up, taps, steps aside, the next arrives. Roughly 1.5–4 seconds apart, and a different order every morning.
  • A stack of cards — one person holds five friends' cards and fans them across the reader. Five different UIDs inside about a second, often in the same order two days running.

A hand cannot produce the second pattern by accident, and a real queue cannot produce it either. That gap is the entire detection idea — which is why the system never accuses anyone, it only flags a run of taps for a human to look at.

04The score is a weighted sum, nothing hidden

Each suspicious run gets up to 100 points. Every signal below is measured on that run alone, multiplied by its weight, and added up. The full table is printed under “show the calculation” on every incident, so any number on this page can be checked by hand.

SignalIn plain EnglishWeight
impossible-gaptwo different cards closer than a hand can move — under 0.5 s0.40
burstmany different cards inside a couple of seconds0.20
crowd-anomalythe mix of people is unusual for that student's history0.12
tight-gaps0.5–1.2 s apart: fast, but a person could do it0.10
order-rigiditythe exact same sequence of cards keeps reappearing0.10
new-cardscards never seen in earlier sessions (could be new students)0.05
nearby-denialsunregistered cards being tried in the same window0.03

One subtraction exists, for fairness: if two students have walked in together for most of the term, their habitual pair gets a small discount so a real friendship is not flagged forever.

07:30:42  CS-06 taps           gap 0.22 s

crowd-anomaly    0.43 × 0.12 = 0.052   this group rarely appears together
tight-gaps       0.00 × 0.10 = 0.000   none in the 0.5–1.2 s band
order-rigidity   0.60 × 0.10 = 0.060   same chain seen 3× before
new-cards        0.00 × 0.05 = 0.000
nearby-denials   0.00 × 0.03 = 0.000

                    raw       0.712  →  71 / 100   HIGH

Read that bottom line as: five different cards were tapped in under a second, in an order that has repeated before, by people who are not usually together. Whether that was a stack of cards or an unusual rush is a judgement call left to a human.

05The four bands, and what to do about them

BandScoreWhat it means
CLEAR0–29nothing unusual in the data
WATCH30–49fast-ish, explainable by a busy door
REVIEW50–69worth a human look at the timestamps
HIGH70+treat as proxy attendance until proven otherwise

The system flags. It never marks anyone absent, never locks anyone out, and never changes a record on its own.

06So what is the Wi-Fi actually for?

Two optional jobs, and neither one is the door:

  • The clock. A freshly booted ESP32 has no idea what year it is. NTP over Wi-Fi turns T+83s into 2026-11-08 11:36:39. With no network there is no real clock, and the log honestly labels those times as relative instead of inventing a date.
  • The uplink. Each record is posted to the laptop, which is what this page reads. With no network the board simply keeps the records in its own flash and uploads them when a network appears.

Everything that matters at the door — reader, roster, LEDs, servo, attendance — runs with the network switched off entirely. If the hotspot is flaky the demo still works; the timestamps are the only thing that gets vaguer.

07What this cannot do

  • It cannot prove who held the card. A friend can tap your card once, slowly, and the data looks perfect.
  • It cannot see a person, so it cannot detect someone slipping through the door behind you.
  • It cannot separate “five friends' cards, stacked” from “five people who happened to arrive at once” with certainty — it can only say the pattern is unusual.

Those limits are the interesting part of the report, not a bug in it. A camera or a second factor would be the next step, and the honest description of this prototype is tamper-evident attendance, not cheat-proof attendance.
